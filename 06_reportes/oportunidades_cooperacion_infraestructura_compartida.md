# Inter-Mine Cooperation Projects & Shared Infrastructure
### Chilean Copper Mining Clusters · March 2026

---

## Introduction

Mining clusters are not just geographic coincidences — they are economic units where proximity
creates the potential for shared infrastructure, joint investment, and operating synergies.
The dashboard identifies 14 production clusters across Chile, many within 50–170 km of each
other. This analysis identifies five geographic cooperation zones, maps the existing and
planned projects already leveraging intra/inter-cluster proximity, and quantifies the potential
scale of cooperation based on dashboard data layers.

Data sources used: desaladoras (17 mining plants + 4 in construction + 3 with EIA approved),
SIGEX exploration registry (931 copper projects across 14 clusters), centrales eléctricas
(large-scale shared generation), cluster proximity matrix, and production forecasts 2026–2032.

## Zone A — Atacama Norte
### Clusters: I-0 (Cerro Colorado) · I-1 (Collahuasi+QB) · II-1 (Centinela) · II-2 (Calama/Codelco) · II-5 (Spence hub)

This is Chile's most densely mined zone and the world's highest concentration of large-scale
copper production. Five clusters sit within a 165 km radius, collectively producing ~2,540 kt/yr
(45% of total modelled Chilean output). The zone spans the Atacama Desert from ~20°S to ~23°S.

**Cluster centroid distances:**
- II-2 ↔ II-1: 15 km (virtually adjacent)
- II-2 ↔ II-5: 62 km
- II-5 ↔ II-1: 66 km
- I-1 ↔ I-0: 81 km
- II-2 ↔ I-1: 159 km

---

### A.1 — Codelco Norte Shared Desalination Plant *(EIA Approved, 2025)*

**Operator:** Codelco
**Capacity:** 1,956 l/s (~61.7 M m³/yr at full flow)
**Location:** Tocopilla coast (~22.2°S, -70.2°W)
**Status:** EIA Aprobado — construction pending
**Clusters served:** II-2 (134 km), II-5 (117 km), II-1 (148 km)

This is the single most consequential planned cooperation infrastructure in Chilean mining.
Codelco's Distrito Norte cluster (Chuquicamata + Radomiro Tomic + Ministro Hales + El Abra +
Gabriela Mistral) currently depends almost entirely on continental groundwater from the
stressed Loa aquifer. The approved plant would supply seawater to Calama (~190 km inland,
~2,300 m elevation), reducing freshwater extraction.

**Scale context:**
- II-2 cluster water demand (2024): ~57 M m³/yr
- Codelco Norte plant capacity: 61.7 M m³/yr → **covers ~108% of II-2's current need**
- Surplus capacity: ~4.7 M m³/yr → available for II-5 or II-1 mines on the same pipeline

**Cross-cluster opportunity:**
The plant is located at Tocopilla, which is also the nearest port for II-5 (117 km) and II-1
(148 km). A shared transmission pipeline along the Tocopilla-Calama corridor could supply
Spence (BHP), Sierra Gorda (KGHM), and Centinela (AMSA) at marginal incremental pipeline
cost. This would transform a single-company investment into a **Zone A water utility**.

**Production forecast link:**
II-2 production declines from 885 kt (2026) to 813 kt (2032) — water demand falls from
56 to 52 M m³/yr. The plant's capacity exceeds need even at peak production, making surplus
sharing economically viable from day one of operation.

### A.2 — Atacama Solar Corridor *(Operational)*

**Relevant plants (all operational, within 150 km of II-2/II-5/II-1 centroids):**

| Plant | Type | MW | Primary cluster | Secondary clusters |
|-------|------|----|-----------------|--------------------|
| CEME 1 | Solar | 401 | II-5 (57 km) | II-2 (68 km), II-1 (82 km) |
| Campos del Sol | Solar | 381 | III-2 (63 km) | III-0 (92 km) |
| Parque Solar Finis Terrae | Solar | 241 | II-5 (55 km) | II-2 (61 km), II-1 (74 km) |
| Sol del Desierto | Solar | 226 | II-2 (66 km) | II-5 (71 km), II-1 (81 km) |
| Taira | Solar | 200 | II-2 (63 km) | II-5 (64 km), II-1 (77 km) |
| Las Salinas Etapa 1 | Solar | 198 | II-5 (33 km) | II-1 (55 km), II-2 (60 km) |
| Coya | Solar | 182 | II-2 (67 km) | II-5 (67 km), II-1 (81 km) |

**Total identified solar capacity within Zone A: >1,800 MW**

The Atacama solar corridor is a de facto shared energy commons. These plants are not
dedicated to any single mining operation — they sell into the SEN (Sistema Eléctrico
Nacional) under PPAs that individual mines purchase. The geographic concentration means
that **any large mine in II-1, II-2, or II-5 draws from the same physical solar generation
assets**, making this the world's largest solar-powered copper mining zone.

**Electricity demand forecast:**
- Zone A total 2026: II-2 (10,615) + II-5 (2,949) + II-1 (2,957) = **16,521 GWh/yr**
- Zone A total 2032: 9,753 + 2,078 + 2,492 = **14,323 GWh/yr** (-13%)

The 1,800+ MW of local solar capacity (~15,768 GWh/yr at 100% capacity factor, realistic
~7,200 GWh/yr at 45% CF) covers **43% of Zone A's 2026 electricity demand** already.
With further solar development — the Atacama has 7-8 kWh/m²/day irradiance, the highest
in the world — the zone could be entirely renewably powered within the forecast horizon.

**Cooperation opportunity:** A joint PPA consortium among Codelco, BHP, AMSA, and KGHM
for Zone A could reduce individual negotiation costs, aggregate demand for longer-term solar
contracts, and fund battery storage for overnight operations. This type of mining energy
cooperative has been explored but not formalized.

### A.3 — Collahuasi + Teck Parallel Desaladoras at Iquique *(I-1, both under construction)*

**Minera Doña Inés de Collahuasi (Collahuasi):**
- Capacity: 1,050 l/s (~33.1 M m³/yr)
- Status: En Construcción (2024)
- Location: -20.808°S, -70.205°W (Iquique coast)

**Minera Teck (Quebrada Blanca QB2):**
- Capacity: 850 l/s (~26.8 M m³/yr)
- Status: En Construcción (2023)
- Location: -20.782°S, -70.188°W (Iquique coast)

These two plants are being built **2.6 km apart on the same stretch of Iquique coastline**,
by two different companies (Glencore/Anglo American for Collahuasi, Teck for QB2), for two
mines that are 50 km apart at high altitude.

**Combined capacity:** 1,900 l/s (~59.9 M m³/yr)

**The cooperation opportunity — and the missed one:**
Building two separate desalination plants at the same coastal point, for mines in the same
geographic cluster, is the clearest example of parallel investment that could instead be a
joint project. Combined, the two plants' economies of scale (plant engineering, coastal land
rights, intake infrastructure, power supply) could have reduced unit CAPEX by an estimated
15–25% (standard desalination scale curves).

The water demand for I-1 at full QB2 capacity is:
- Collahuasi (559 kt × 93 m³/t): ~52.0 M m³/yr
- QB2 (330 kt target × 93 m³/t): ~30.7 M m³/yr
- **Total I-1 demand: ~82.7 M m³/yr**

The combined capacity of both plants (59.9 M m³/yr) covers **72% of full-capacity demand**
— still a gap even with both plants running. A joint third-phase expansion, shared between
both operators, could fill the remaining 28%.

**Precedent for cooperation:** Collahuasi and QB operate in the same region, serve the same
export corridor (Iquique port), and face the same altitude/temperature challenges for pipeline
design. A Collahuasi-Teck joint water committee already reportedly shares technical data for
environmental impact assessments.

## Zone B — Escondida–Spence Corridor
### Clusters: II-0 (Escondida+Zaldívar) · II-5 (Spence+Sierra Gorda+Lomas Bayas)

**Centroid separation: 167 km**

Both clusters are owned largely by BHP (Escondida in II-0, Spence in II-5), making this
zone the most advanced in terms of actual cross-cluster cooperation.

---

### B.1 — BHP Shared Desalination System: EWS + SGO *(Fully operational)*

**BHP EWS (Escondida Water Supply):**
- Capacity: 3,858 l/s (~121.7 M m³/yr at 90% utilisation)
- Status: Operational since 2017-18
- Location: Antofagasta coast (-23.750°S, -70.467°W)
- Primary beneficiary: Escondida (II-0)

**BHP SGO (Spence Growth Option desaladora):**
- Capacity: 1,000 l/s (~31.5 M m³/yr)
- Status: Operational since 2021
- Location: Mejillones area (-23.037°S, -70.343°W)
- Primary beneficiary: Spence (II-5)

These are two separate BHP plants on the same 167 km coastal stretch, serving mines in
two different clusters. Because both are BHP assets, water allocation across the system
is managed centrally by BHP Chile's water operations group — making this the **most
explicit inter-cluster water sharing arrangement in Chile**.

**Operational cooperation details:**
- During Escondida production dips (e.g., 2021: 1,011 kt), EWS capacity surplus can
  be reallocated to Spence via the pipeline network. BHP's integrated water management
  allows dynamic balancing between the two mines.
- Combined BHP desalination capacity in Zone B: **4,858 l/s (~153.2 M m³/yr)**
- Zone B water demand (2024): Escondida ~119 M m³/yr + Spence ~21 M m³/yr = ~140 M m³/yr
- **Coverage ratio: 109%** — BHP already exceeds its own Zone B demand

**Production forecast implication:**
- II-0 production declines from 1,425 kt (2026) to 1,097 kt (2032) → water demand:
  128 → 97 M m³/yr
- II-5 declines from 246 → 173 kt → demand: 23 → 16 M m³/yr
- By 2032, combined BHP Zone B water demand: ~113 M m³/yr
- BHP desalination capacity: 153 M m³/yr (unchanged)
- **Surplus by 2032: ~40 M m³/yr** — equivalent to supplying Sierra Gorda (KGHM) and
  Lomas Bayas (Glencore) entirely from BHP's system, which could be offered commercially

**The "small victory":** Declining production creates capacity headroom in BHP's desalination
system that could serve third-party mines — turning an infrastructure cost into a revenue line.

### B.2 — FCAB Railway: Zone B–A Shared Logistics Backbone *(Operational)*

The Ferrocarril de Antofagasta a Bolivia (FCAB) is the pre-existing shared infrastructure
that connects both zones. From Antofagasta port, the main line runs to Calama (the II-2
hub), with branch lines serving Spence (II-5), Escondida (via spur near Sierra Gorda),
and the Atacama Norte corridor.

**Key flows on the FCAB relevant to Zones A+B:**
- Sulfuric acid (for oxide leaching): imported via Antofagasta, distributed by rail to
  II-0 (Zaldívar), II-2 (El Abra, RT), and II-5 (Sierra Gorda)
- Copper cathodes: outbound from II-2 (Codelco oxide mines), II-0 (Zaldívar)
- Diesel and reagents: inbound for all Zone A+B operations

The FCAB serves as a **neutral common carrier** — the most mature form of inter-company
logistics cooperation in Chilean mining. Multiple competing companies (BHP, AMSA, KGHM,
Codelco) all rely on the same rail infrastructure, coordinated by FCAB.

**Cooperation gap:** Escondida's concentrate goes via slurry pipeline to Coloso port, bypassing
FCAB. A joint pipeline-to-port arrangement for the II-5 cluster (Spence concentrate currently
trucked 118 km to Tocopilla) could reduce logistics costs and road wear for the entire zone.
This remains a feasibility-study candidate, not yet under active development.

## Zone C — Atacama Sur
### Clusters: III-0 (Salvador+Mantoverde) · III-1 (Capstone/Candelaria) · III-2 (Candelaria+Caserones)

**Separation: III-1 ↔ III-2: 77 km; III-2 ↔ III-0: 143 km**

This zone comprises the Atacama Region's (Region III) mining complex, dominated by Lundin
Mining (Candelaria, Caserones), Capstone Copper (Mantoverde), and Codelco (Salvador). Combined
production ~480 kt/yr. The zone contains the highest density of exploration activity in Chile
after Region II (141 SIGEX copper projects in III-0 alone).

---

### C.1 — Atacama Sur Desalination Cluster *(Operational + under construction)*

Four desalination plants already operate or are under construction in a 75 km coastal strip
between Caldera (-27.1°S) and Huasco (-28.5°S):

| Plant | Operator | Capacity (l/s) | Status | Mine served |
|-------|----------|---------------|--------|-------------|
| Lundin Mining | Candelaria | 500 | Operational (2013) | Candelaria (III-2) |
| AGUASCAP (CAP) | Mixed | 600 | Operational (2015) | Shared — CAP steel + mining |
| Minera Mantos Copper | Capstone | 120 | Operational (2014) | Mantoverde (III-1) |
| Capstone Expansión | Capstone | 260 | En Construcción (2024) | Mantoverde expansion |

**Combined operational capacity: 1,220 l/s (~38.5 M m³/yr)**
**With Capstone expansion: 1,480 l/s (~46.7 M m³/yr)**

The AGUASCAP plant (operated by CAP, the Chilean steel company) is the most interesting
case — it is a **multi-sector shared plant** that supplies water to the CAP Atacama steel
complex AND sells capacity to nearby mining operations. This is one of the few operational
examples of industrial water sharing between different economic sectors in Chile.

**Water demand of Zone C:**
- Candelaria (sulfuros, 123 kt/yr × 93 m³/t): 11.4 M m³/yr
- Caserones (sulfuros, 126 kt/yr × 93 m³/t): 11.7 M m³/yr
- Mantoverde/Capstone (~103 kt/yr × 60 m³/t mixed): 6.2 M m³/yr
- Salvador (~50 kt/yr × 80 m³/t smelter): 4.0 M m³/yr
- **Zone C total: ~33 M m³/yr**

Zone C's 46.7 M m³/yr capacity (post-expansion) **exceeds current demand by 42%**. The surplus
is a tangible cooperation opportunity: the three operator groups (Lundin, Capstone, Codelco/
Salvador) could formalize a water-sharing agreement that eliminates individual freshwater
permits in the Atacama Region, removing a key permitting bottleneck.

**Salvador opportunity note:**
Codelco's Salvador mine (III-0) is 143 km from the Candelaria/Capstone desalination
cluster — too far for a direct pipeline at current scale. However, as Codelco's Norte
desaladora (Zone A, 1,956 l/s) advances, the question of a southern Codelco desaladora for
Salvador will arise. The Zone C cluster represents an alternative: purchasing water from the
existing III-1/III-2 plants rather than building new.

### C.2 — Nueva Unión Joint Venture *(Feasibility stage)*

**Partners:** Codelco (50%) + Teck (50%)
**Asset:** La Unión-Relincho copper-molybdenum deposit, Region III
**Location:** ~28.8°S, -71.4°W (between III-1 and IV-2 clusters, 111 km from III-1)
**Planned desaladora:** 970 l/s (~30.6 M m³/yr), EIA en elaboración
**Status:** Feasibility study underway

Nueva Unión is the largest-scale formal inter-company cooperation project in Zone C (and
arguably in Chilean mining broadly). A Codelco-Teck 50/50 JV was established to develop
a world-class porphyry deposit that neither company could finance alone at current copper
prices.

**Why this matters for the cluster analysis:**
- The deposit sits between clusters III-1 and IV-2, in a zone not yet assigned to any
  existing cluster. Its development would create a **new cluster** in the Region III–IV
  border area.
- The planned 970 l/s desaladora would be the largest desalination investment in Zone C,
  exceeding the combined existing capacity (1,220 l/s). Its coastal location would also
  bring desalination infrastructure to a currently underserved coastal stretch.
- Production scenarios (not yet in Produccion_Master.csv) suggest 300–500 kt/yr at full
  capacity — which would shift the Zone C cluster balance substantially.

**Cross-cluster spillover:**
With Teck already operating QB2 in I-1 (Zone A) and Candelaria being Lundin/Candelaria-zone
adjacent, Nueva Unión's desaladora could theoretically supply water to both Caserones (III-2,
160 km) and the new deposit simultaneously — creating a Zone C water backbone.

---

### C.3 — ENAMI Smelter at Paipote (III-0) — Zone C Processing Hub *(Operational)*

ENAMI's Fundición Paipote (near Copiapó, Region III) is a state-operated custom smelter
that processes concentrates from **multiple small and medium mines across Zones C and E**.
It is the most explicit form of processing cooperation in Chilean mining: private junior
miners ship their concentrate to a neutral state facility rather than each building their
own smelter.

**Mines that regularly use ENAMI Paipote:**
- Small miners in III-0 corridor (Atacama Region artisanal and small-scale)
- Potential processing fallback for Salvador (Codelco) during smelter maintenance
- Regional juniors and explorers who lack independent processing capacity

**Relevance to cooperation:** ENAMI's model — a neutral processing hub that serves
multiple competitors — is a template for future water, rail, or power cooperation in
Zone C. The operational precedent reduces the political/competitive barriers to shared
infrastructure in other dimensions.

## Zone D — Central Andean Corridor
### Clusters: RM-0 (Los Bronces) · V-1 (Andina) · VI-0 (El Teniente)

**Separation: RM-0 ↔ V-1: 2 km; VI-0 ↔ RM-0: 114 km**

The near-zero RM-0/V-1 separation reflects that Los Bronces (Anglo American) and Andina
(Codelco) are separated by only the Aconcagua mountain range. Both operate at 3,000–4,500 m
elevation in the Santiago Andes, drawing water from Andean tributaries and sharing the
same power grid infrastructure.

---

### D.1 — Shared Hydro Power: Alfalfal + Las Lajas Complex *(Operational)*

The Central Andean corridor is served by a cluster of high-head run-of-river hydro plants
that act as shared generation for all three clusters:

| Plant | MW | Distance to VI-0 | Distance to RM-0/V-1 |
|-------|----|-----------------|-----------------------|
| Las Lajas | 277 | 73 km | 43 km |
| Alfalfal II | 259 | 73 km | 45 km |
| Alfalfal | 185 | 82 km | 38 km |
| Candelaria thermal | 238 | 16 km | 100 km |

**Combined hydro capacity near Zone D: ~720 MW (~6,307 GWh/yr at 100% CF)**

These plants feed directly into the SIC/SEN grid segments that also supply Los Bronces,
Andina, and El Teniente. While not contractually dedicated to mining, the geographic proximity
means Zone D mines are the primary load centers for this hydro generation — a de facto
cooperation arrangement mediated by the grid.

**Climate vulnerability:** The 2016–2021 megadrought reduced Andean reservoir and
snowpack levels by 30–50% in this zone, forcing Zone D mines to rely on thermal backup.
The shared dependence on the same Andean watershed means **all three clusters face the
same climate risk simultaneously** — making a joint adaptation strategy (demand response,
shared backup thermal capacity, water recycling) more efficient than individual solutions.

**Zone D electricity demand:**
- RM-0 + V-1 (~2 km apart): 2,413 + 2,279 = 4,692 GWh/yr (2026 forecast)
- VI-0: 3,240 GWh/yr
- **Total Zone D: ~7,932 GWh/yr** — largely served by the Andean hydro complex above

---

### D.2 — Anglo American Sur: Los Bronces + El Soldado Shared Services *(Operational)*

Anglo American operates Los Bronces (RM-0) and El Soldado (V-0, adjacent to V-1) as the
"Anglo American Sur" business unit — a formal intra-company multi-mine management structure.

**Shared services under Anglo American Sur:**
- Centralised maintenance and spare-parts inventory (reducing idle capital per mine)
- Shared environmental monitoring network for the Aconcagua and Maipo watersheds
- Common community relations program for Santiago Andes communities
- Shared reagents procurement contracts (economy of scale across two mines)

**Cross-cluster water risk:** Both Los Bronces and Andina (V-1, Codelco) draw from the
same upper Maipo and Aconcagua watershed. The two mines are competitors but face identical
water security challenges. An informal coordination mechanism exists through the Santiago
Basin Water Committee (Mesa de Agua del Maipo) — a government-facilitated but
industry-funded inter-sector water committee that includes mining, agriculture, and urban
water users.

## Zone E — Coquimbo–Aconcagua
### Clusters: IV-1 (Los Pelambres) · IV-2 (Andacollo)

**Centroid separation: 179 km**

---

### E.1 — Los Pelambres Desaladora + Teck Andacollo *(IV-1 En Construcción, IV-2 Teck-owned)*

**Minera Los Pelambres desaladora:**
- Capacity: 400 l/s (~12.6 M m³/yr)
- Status: En Construcción (2023)
- Location: Los Vilos coast (-31.882°S, -71.497°W)
- Distance from IV-2 centroid: 188 km (too far for direct pipeline to Andacollo)

**Current IV-2 water supply:** Teck's Andacollo mine draws from local water rights in
the Coquimbo Region. Production is ~30 kt/yr (mixed sulfuros/óxidos, ~65 m³/t), implying
~2 M m³/yr water demand — manageable on local sources.

**The cooperation opportunity:**
The Los Pelambres desaladora is sized at 400 l/s, but Los Pelambres' current demand
at 331 kt/yr is ~31 M m³/yr (equivalently ~983 l/s continuous flow). The 400 l/s plant
covers only **40% of current demand** — it is designed to supplement river water, not
replace it.

As climate change reduces Choapa River flow and community pressure intensifies, Los
Pelambres will need to expand its coastal water supply. A shared pipeline along the
Coquimbo coast from Los Vilos northward could eventually reach Andacollo (40 km from
Coquimbo), creating a Zone E water corridor. Given Teck's experience building QB2's
4,800 m elevation pipeline in Zone A, the technical capability exists; what is missing
is the commercial framework.

---

### E.2 — Nueva Unión as Zone E–C Bridge *(Feasibility stage)*

Nueva Unión's location at ~28.8°S places it geographically between Zone C (III-1 cluster
at ~28.0°S) and Zone E (IV-2 at ~30.2°S). If developed, it would:
- Connect Zone C's desalination infrastructure southward (970 l/s plant)
- Provide a logical intermediate point for a Coquimbo-to-Atacama water utility corridor
- Create shared logistics (road, potentially rail) between Region III and IV copper districts

**Why SIGEX data confirms this:** Both Codelco and AMSA have SIGEX exploration concessions
in both IV-1 and IV-2, suggesting each company is mapping a connected mineral corridor between
Coquimbo and Atacama — exactly the zone Nueva Unión occupies.

## Forecast Implications for Cooperation Projects

The production decline forecast (model v7, 2026–2032) creates a specific economic logic
for cooperation:

**Declining production = excess infrastructure capacity**
- BHP's Zone B desalination surplus grows from ~13 M m³/yr (2026) to ~40 M m³/yr (2032)
- Zone A Atacama solar surplus increases as II-2 and II-5 production falls
- El Teniente's SIC grid connection becomes increasingly oversized

**Implication:** Companies will have incentive to monetize excess shared infrastructure
capacity by selling or leasing to third parties. The infrastructure cooperation projects
that are most commercially viable are those that **convert declining mines' fixed asset
surplus into revenues** — water utilities, power resale, and logistics services.

**Rising production = new infrastructure investment needed**
- I-1 (Collahuasi + QB2 ramp): genuine new demand for water, power, logistics. Zone A
  cooperation is attractive precisely because QB2's expansion needs what the zone already has.
- Nueva Unión (Zone C/E bridge): if developed, creates new demand that justifies the Zone C
  desalination corridor as a commercial water utility.

## Key Findings

**1. Zone A is the cooperation nucleus of Chilean copper.** Five clusters, four major companies,
and three planned/approved desalination projects within 165 km create the conditions for a
formal Atacama mining infrastructure utility — particularly for water and solar energy.
Codelco's 1,956 l/s Norte desaladora is the anchor.

**2. The most mature cooperation is BHP intra-company (Zone B).** BHP's cross-cluster EWS+SGO
water sharing demonstrates that inter-cluster infrastructure is operationally feasible. The
barrier to extending this to third parties (KGHM, Glencore) is commercial, not technical.

**3. Zone C has a structural processing hub (ENAMI Paipote) but fragmented water supply.**
The convergence of Lundin, Capstone, and Codelco assets in a 143 km zone, each building
separate desalination plants, is an opportunity for consolidation. Nueva Unión would amplify
Zone C's water infrastructure scale.

**4. Collahuasi and QB are building two plants 2.6 km apart.** This is the clearest missed
cooperation opportunity in the dataset — parallel investment by two companies at the same
coastal location, for two mines in the same cluster. Even at this late stage (both under
construction), a shared intake structure, permitting process, and discharge management could
reduce ongoing OPEX.

**5. Declining production does not reduce the case for cooperation — it strengthens it.**
As individual mines' production falls, unit costs rise for fixed infrastructure (pipelines,
plants, rail). Sharing fixed costs across more users reduces the per-tonne burden for
everyone. The 2026–2032 forecast window is precisely when cooperation economics become
most compelling.
